## Roku Stick vs Roku Ultra: The quick answer

- **HD TV, tight budget:** an entry-level Roku Express-style player.
- **4K TV, most homes:** a 4K streaming stick. It's the best balance for most people.
- **4K TV, weak Wi-Fi or a home theater:** a Roku Ultra, mainly for its Ethernet port and extras.

Roku changes its lineup and names fairly often, so check the current model page before you buy. An older "best Roku" article may describe models no longer on sale.

## How the types compare

| Feature | Express-class | 4K stick | Ultra |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Top resolution | HD on the base model; 4K on "4K" versions | 4K HDR | 4K HDR |
| Form | Small box that needs line of sight or a clear path for its remote, depending on the model | Stick that plugs directly into HDMI | Larger box |
| Wired internet | Generally needs an adapter | Generally needs an adapter | Built-in Ethernet |
| Remote | Basic | Voice remote | Upgraded voice remote with extras such as a remote finder |
| Best for | Secondary HD TVs | Main TV in most homes | Main TV with demanding needs |

Exact features vary by model and year, so compare the spec sheet of the exact one you're considering.

## What really makes a difference

### Roku Stick vs Roku Ultra: Your TV's resolution

A 4K player only makes a visible difference on a 4K TV. On an older 1080p bedroom TV, a basic player does the job, though a 4K model can still be worth it if you plan to move it to a newer TV later.

### Your Wi-Fi

This matters more than most specs. If your router is far away, or your apartment has many competing networks, buffering is more likely on a low-end player. If you can run a cable to the TV, the Ultra's Ethernet port is the simplest fix. Its port is 10/100, which still comfortably handles a 4K stream.

### Speed

Budget players take longer to open apps and scroll through menus. If you use the TV every day, the small price step to a faster stick usually pays off. You'll notice it more than any picture setting.

### The remote

Voice search, TV power and volume buttons save you from juggling two remotes. Check that the model you're buying includes them, because the base remote on entry-level players can be simpler.

## Stick or box?

A stick hides behind the TV and draws power through a USB cable or adapter. A box sits in the open, which can help airflow and Wi-Fi reception and gives you room for extra ports. If your TV is wall-mounted with little space behind it, measure first, or use an HDMI extender so the stick isn't pressed against the wall.

## Which Dolby formats do you need?

If your TV supports Dolby Vision, or you have a Dolby Atmos soundbar, confirm that the player supports those too. Support tends to be on 4K models, but it varies, so check the spec sheet.

## Where Roku falls short

Roku's simplicity comes from a closed app store. That's a drawback if you want IPTV players or other apps Roku doesn't carry. If that matters to you, read our [Roku vs Fire Stick comparison](/compare/roku-vs-fire-stick) and our guide to [what works for IPTV on Roku](/setup/roku) before you buy.


When choosing a reliable **Roku Stick vs Roku Ultra**, verifying network peering and server routing ensures optimal playback stability.
